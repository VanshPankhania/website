How the arm works

This 6 degrees of freedom (DOF) robotic arm is designed to mimic the kinematics of a human arm, allowing it to reach any point within its workspace with a specific orientation. Each joint contributes one rotational degree of freedom, enabling the arm to position and orient its end-effector (gripper) in three-dimensional space.

The system combines mechanical design, servo-based actuation and Arduino-driven control logic to achieve smooth, coordinated motion.

1. Kinematic structure

The arm consists of six rotational joints arranged in a configuration similar to an industrial manipulator:

JointMotionFunction
J1 – Base rotationYawRotates the entire arm left and right.
J2 – ShoulderPitchRaises and lowers the main arm segment.
J3 – ElbowPitchExtends or retracts the arm's reach.
J4 – Wrist rollRollRotates the wrist around the arm's axis.
J5 – Wrist pitchPitchControls the up and down angle of the wrist.
J6 – Gripper / wrist yawYaw or gripRotates the end-effector or opens and closes the gripper.

This arrangement allows the arm to perform full 3D positioning and orientation control, similar to a human shoulder, elbow and wrist system.

2. Actuation system (PWM servos)

The arm uses PWM hobby servos, each providing:

  • Built-in position control
  • Torque suitable for lightweight 3D-printed mechanisms
  • A simple 3-wire interface (power, ground, signal)

The Arduino Uno R3 generates PWM signals using the Servo.h library, sending each servo a pulse between 1000 and 2000 µs, corresponding to its angular position.

To ensure smooth motion:

  • Movements are interpolated using small incremental steps
  • Delays are added between steps to prevent jerky motion
  • Dead-zones are filtered out to avoid micro-oscillations

This results in stable, fluid joint movement, even with low-cost servos.

3. Mechanical design (3D-printed parts)

All structural components are 3D-printed, including:

  • Base housing
  • Shoulder and elbow brackets
  • Wrist assembly
  • Servo mounts
  • Gripper mechanism

The design focuses on:

  • Lightweight geometry to reduce servo load
  • Reinforced stress points around joints
  • Modular assembly, allowing easy replacement or upgrades
  • Cable routing channels to keep wiring tidy

PLA or PETG is typically used for rigidity and ease of printing.

4. Electronics and control

4.1 Arduino Uno R3

The Arduino acts as the central controller, responsible for:

  • Generating PWM signals
  • Reading input devices (joystick, potentiometers, serial commands, etc.)
  • Executing programmed motion sequences

4.2 Power distribution

Servos draw significant current, so the system uses:

  • A dedicated 5–6 V external power supply for the servos
  • A common ground between the Arduino and servo power
  • Capacitors to smooth voltage spikes

This prevents brown-outs and ensures consistent torque.

5. Software and motion logic

The Arduino code handles:

5.1 Manual control

Using serial commands, joysticks, or even by moving your own arm, each joint can be moved individually.

5.2 Automated sequences

Pre-programmed routines allow the arm to:

  • Perform pick-and-place cycles
  • Move through waypoints
  • Demonstrate gestures or patterns

5.3 Motion smoothing

To avoid sudden jumps:

  • Each target angle is reached gradually
  • Speed is controlled by step size
  • Easing functions can be added for natural movement

5.4 Safety limits

Software-defined angle limits prevent:

  • Over-rotation
  • Mechanical collisions
  • Servo strain

6. End-effector (gripper)

The gripper is servo-driven and designed to:

  • Open and close smoothly
  • Grip small objects
  • Maintain alignment during wrist rotation

Alternative end-effectors (suction, magnetic, tool-holders) can be added thanks to the modular wrist design.

7. System capabilities

The completed robotic arm needs to be able to:

  • Reach and orient objects within its workspace
  • Perform repeatable pick-and-place tasks
  • Demonstrate human-like arm motion
  • Execute programmed routines
  • Interface with external systems (PC, sensors, cameras)

Its modular design makes it ideal for:

  • Engineering coursework
  • Robotics research
  • Automation experiments
  • Gesture-controlled or vision-guided upgrades

Design process

Defining the requirements

Before I started any CAD work, I needed a clear idea of what the robotic arm should actually be able to do. I took a lot of inspiration from a Brazio Robotics model because I liked how sleek, modular and straightforward their design was. Using that as a reference point, I sketched out the basic capabilities I wanted: a full 6-DOF layout, each joint driven by a servo with 180 degrees of motion, and enough strength to lift a useful payload without overstressing the structure.

The servos I chose are rated at 20 kg·cm, which sounds huge at first, but in practice it means they can hold 20 kg at a 1 cm radius. Using the torque relationship:

Load = 20 kg·cm ÷ lever arm
Load = 20 kg·cm ÷ 10 cm ≈ 2 kg

There is about a 10 cm distance from the wrist joint to the gripper, which gives a theoretical payload of about 2 kg. Once you factor in real-world losses, material flex and safety margins, the realistic working payload ends up closer to 1–1.2 kg. Having these numbers in mind early on helped shape the rest of the design and kept everything grounded in what the arm could reliably achieve.

Modelling in Fusion 360

Once the functional requirements were set, I moved into modelling the arm in Fusion 360. I built each joint, bracket and linkage using reference dimensions and kept everything parametric so I could easily tweak tolerances or geometry later on. Using constraints helped maintain alignment and symmetry across the whole arm, which made the design feel cleaner and more modular. I added fillets and chamfers to reduce stress concentrations on load-bearing parts, especially around servo mounts and rotating joints.

Throughout the process, I used Blender's armature tools, as I was already familiar with Blender, to simulate how the arm would move, making sure each axis behaved correctly before committing to printing anything.

Preparing for printing

After the CAD models were finished, I prepared them for 3D printing by exporting STL files with a suitable mesh resolution and orienting each part to maximise layer strength along the directions that would experience the most load. I added supports only where absolutely necessary to keep post-processing simple. For slicing, I used a 0.2–0.28 mm layer height, which gives a good balance between print quality and strength when working with PETG.

Printing in PETG

I printed the parts in PETG because it offers strong layer adhesion, good impact resistance and low brittleness, which are ideal qualities for a mechanical arm. Using the Creality K2, I ran the nozzle at around 250–260 °C and the bed at 70–80 °C, with slower print speeds between 40–60 mm/s to reduce stringing. Once printed, I checked each part for warping, dimensional accuracy and overall print quality before moving on to assembly.
